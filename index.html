Survey Scan

Python C++ ROS 2 MoveIt OpenCV YOLO26 Roboflow Point Clouds
Watch Demo

What

The survey scan takes a board lying anywhere in the work area and produces a 3D scan of it. The operator only has to pick which board. Down the line, the goal is to measure each board's size and position accurately enough to saw or split it precisely to match the plans. The point clouds could also be used to train a model that decides where each board is best used, based on how warped or defective it is. A straight board with little warp suits a wall frame's base plate, for example, while a heavily warped one is better cut into short blocking pieces between studs.

How

Step 1 of 9

Recognise the boards

The survey starts by finding boards in a camera image with a segmentation model I trained.

A segmentation model outlines every pixel of a board, where a detection model would only draw a box around it. Those exact pixels matter, because they're used to pull the board's depth out of the depth image.

The training images came from the robot's own camera. I added a capture mode that moves it through survey-like poses at different tilts and saves each frame. I labelled each board's top face as a polygon in Roboflow, exported the set in YOLO format, and fine-tuned a COCO-pretrained YOLO26n-seg model on it. I also trained YOLOv8n-seg on the same data to compare, and kept YOLO26n.

Frames taken back to back are nearly identical, so the train/validation split keeps those near-duplicates on the same side. Otherwise the validation score would make the model look better than it is.

Step 2 of 9

Capture a clean depth image

The arm parks at a survey pose that looks over the work area, and the camera takes a short burst of frames.

The depth images are merged by taking each pixel's median. Stereo depth flickers from frame to frame, and the noise grows with distance. The arm is still at this point, so combining frames gives a cleaner depth image without slowing anything down.

Step 3 of 9

Fit the board's geometry

Each outline is turned into a 3D position, direction and size.

  • Every pixel inside the mask is back-projected into a 3D point using its depth and the camera's intrinsics.
  • Stray points far from the rest are dropped.
  • A plane is fitted to what's left with SVD, then refitted a few times, each time discarding points that sit off it. This removes edge pixels that landed on the floor.
  • PCA on the remaining points gives the board's axes. The direction with the most spread is its length, the one with the least is the face normal, and the third runs across its width.
  • Length and width are the spread of the points along those axes, trimmed slightly so a few outliers don't stretch them.
  • The ratio of the two largest spreads shows how sure the fit is about the long axis. A nearly square patch can't give a reliable direction.
  • Thickness is the median height of the top face above a thin ring of floor just around the mask.

Step 4 of 9

Convert to robot coordinates

These measurements are in the camera's frame, but the robot plans in its own base frame.

The conversion chains two transforms: the arm's live tool pose, which the robot reports, and the camera's position on the wrist, which comes from hand-eye calibration.

I automated that calibration:

  • The operator points the camera at a checkerboard.
  • The routine generates candidate poses by tilting about an axis through the board and rolling about the lens axis, which keeps the board centred in view.
  • It picks the reachable candidates that differ most from each other, and records an image and the tool pose at each one.
  • solvePnP finds the board's pose in every image.
  • OpenCV's hand-eye solver (Park's method) solves for the camera-to-tool transform.

Step 5 of 9

Pick a board and correct its height

The operator picks a board on the screen.

At this distance, depth is least reliable for height and tilt, but the boards always lie on a known surface: the floor, wood supports, or a custom height set for the run. So the controller:

  • places the board's top at that surface height plus the board's thickness;
  • slides the measured centre along the camera's line of sight to that height, which keeps the board where the camera saw it in the image;
  • lays the board flat.

Step 6 of 9

Take a close look

Even after that correction, the survey position can be several centimetres off.

That's enough for a sweep to run along the board's edge instead of its middle. So the camera moves directly above the board, and a separate close-range model segments the top face:

  • The points are converted into the robot's frame.
  • An SVD of their horizontal positions gives the heading.
  • The outermost points across that heading give the two long edges. An edge only counts if the mask doesn't touch the image border there, since that would mean the board is cut off.

The controller then moves the centre line, heading and width onto what it measured.

Step 7 of 9

Sweep the board

The arm moves the camera along the board in up to three passes: one over the top, and one along each side with the camera turned to face it, to capture the faces a top view can't see.

Before each pass, the controller checks reach:

  • It samples poses along the line, solves inverse kinematics at each, and keeps the longest continuous reachable stretch.
  • It narrows each boundary by bisection.
  • MoveIt plans the straight-line path. If the path stops short, the end is pulled in and it tries again.
  • Side passes are walked in small steps, with each IK solve seeded from the previous step's joints. They stop at the first collision, missing solution, or sudden joint jump, which would mean the arm flipping posture mid-sweep.

This way a board that's partly out of reach still gets scanned over the part the arm can reach. The board itself goes into MoveIt's collision scene as a padded box, so the camera and gripper are planned around it.

Step 8 of 9

Check the ends

At the end of each pass, the camera checks whether it's looking at the real end of the board.

A mask can stop early because of a shadow, and the board can run past where the survey thought it ended. The check:

  • fits the face's plane in the robot frame;
  • finds the last column of the mask along the board;
  • makes sure the whole width is in view;
  • looks just past that column, where the depth has to drop off the face onto the background in two separate frames.

If the face's depth instead runs right to the image border on the same plane, the board continues, and the arm steps further along until it finds the end or reaches a set limit.

Step 9 of 9

Build the point clouds

The arm keeps moving while frames come in, so each frame is paired with the arm's pose at the moment it was captured.

The camera's timestamp is converted to the computer's clock, and the tool pose is interpolated between the robot's samples at that instant. Each frame's masked depth is then back-projected, transformed into the robot frame, and merged into point clouds of the top and sides.

Small calibration errors still leave seams between passes. So a second, aligned cloud keeps the top fixed and moves each side pass as one rigid piece, positioned to close the gap at the shared edge slice by slice along the board. Working slice by slice preserves the real shape of a twisted or bowed board.

Pick and Place

C++ ROS 2 MoveIt Python FastAPI Next.js
Watch Demo

What

Pick and place builds on the same survey to sort boards. The operator ticks several boards in the survey view, and the robot picks them up one at a time and lays them in a row on the floor, shortest first.

How

Step 1 of 5

Measure every board

The survey measures lengths from across the work area, which isn't precise enough to put boards of similar length in order.

So before touching anything, the camera visits both ends of every board and runs the same end check as the scan: it finds where the face mask stops, confirmed by depth falling off the face. If the board runs past the view, the camera steps outward and looks again. The two ends give each board's length, its heading (the line between them) and its centre (their midpoint).

Step 2 of 5

Plan the layout

With those measurements, the controller works out a spot for every board, side by side in a fixed sorting zone, and checks them all before the first pick.

Each footprint is grown by a clearance margin and tested against the others, and against the boards still waiting, with a separating-axis test, which handles rectangles at any angle. That way, a board that won't fit is caught before anything moves.

Step 3 of 5

Plan the grip

Right before each grip, the camera takes one more close look at the board, since it may have been nudged, and the grip is planned from what that look finds.

The jaws close along a level line across the board at its centre, with the tool pointing straight down. Two wrist angles 180° apart give the same grip, and the controller picks the one closer to the wrist's current angle so the wrist doesn't turn more than it has to.

The grip point is measured from the gripper's collision model. The controller works out how far the gripper's shapes reach below the flange, so the target sits at the real fingertips even after the gripper attachments change. How far the jaws go down the board comes from its thickness.

Step 4 of 5

Approach and close

The arm moves to a pose above the board and checks that it actually arrived.

It then descends in a straight line planned by MoveIt, so the fingers come straight down without dragging across the wood. After another position check, the controller closes the gripper through the UR's digital outputs.

Step 5 of 5

Carry and place

Once gripped, the board is attached to the gripper in MoveIt's planning scene, so the carry is planned with the board's size included.

The arm lifts the board, carries it to its spot, lowers it to just above the floor, lets go, and lifts clear. Each placed board then becomes an obstacle for the ones after it.

AMR Rack Delivery

MiR AMRs MiR API Allen-Bradley PLC Studio 5000 Ladder Logic Banner DXM100 AprilTags
Watch Demo

What

A system that moves racks of door parts around the plant with MiR robots. Operators call a robot with a button, and it carries full racks from the injection molding machine to the drop-off lanes, brings empty racks back to the machine, and delivers racks to other parts of the plant. I built all of it: the changes to the racks, the robot's map and missions, the PLC logic and buttons that call it, and the overhead camera that tells it where to drop each rack.

How

Step 1 of 7

Ready the racks

The robot tows racks with a hook, so each rack needed a bar to hook onto and a marker the robot can recognize.

With the plant's kaizen (continuous improvement) team, I added hook bars to the racks and frames to hold markers. I set up and printed the AprilTags that go in those frames, which the robot's camera reads to identify each rack and line up with it. I also set the hook and rack sizes in the robot so it grips and tows each rack correctly.

Step 2 of 7

Map the plant

Before the robot can drive anywhere on its own, it needs a map of the floor.

I drove the robot around the plant to build its map, then set the positions and docking points it uses at the molding machine and the drop-off lanes. On the map I drew zones for areas it has to stay out of, speed limits, preferred paths, and spots where it warns people with sounds and lights. I set up its charging too.

I also set and adjusted its protective fields, the zones around the robot where its laser scanners make it slow down or stop if something gets too close. I also replaced the scanners a few times when they broke.

Step 3 of 7

Write the missions

Each job the robot does is a mission, a sequence of moves and actions I programmed.

I wrote missions to carry full racks from the molding machine to the drop-off lanes, bring empty racks back to the machine, and deliver racks to other parts of the plant. When the robot gets stuck, the mission retries, and when it fails, the operators get an alert.

Step 4 of 7

Call the robot with a button

Operators start a mission by pressing a wireless button.

A Banner DXM100, a wireless controller wired into the PLC's inputs, receives each press and passes it to the PLC. Ladder logic I wrote queues the requests when several come in close together, then starts the matching mission on the robot through MiR's API.

Step 5 of 7

Pick a drop-off spot

Full racks are dropped off in lanes on the floor, and the robot needs to know where the next free spot is.

For each lane, the vision program sends the PLC the outermost free spot, the one the robot should drive to. PLC logic I wrote picks which lane the rack goes into, starting from the lane at the top of the camera's view, and sends the robot to that spot.

The vision program also sends a heartbeat, a signal that flips on every update. If it stops, the PLC switches the robot back to its old way of finding a spot, driving up to each lane and checking each spot with its own sensors.

Step 6 of 7

Show the robot's status

I built an HMI screen for the system.

It shows the robot's status, its current mission and any faults, so operators can see what it's doing.

Step 7 of 7

Plan the layout and roll it out

I planned where the robot picks up and drops off racks and the paths it drives between them.

I worked with team members and operators to find the layout that worked best for production. Then I tested and commissioned the system on the line, trained the operators, wrote work instructions, and supported it once it was running.

Impact

In production the system handled over 100 rack moves a shift across all its missions. That took the job of moving racks off the operators and cut the production delays that came with it. I also tracked how the system was used, including how many racks were moved in total and of each type, how far the robot travelled, and how long it spent on missions versus sitting idle.

Drop-off Vision System

Python OpenCV NumPy RTSP Streaming Multithreading EtherNet/IP
Watch Demo View Code

Bird's-eye detection output

Live calibration interface

What

The overhead camera system that tells the robot where to drop each rack. A Python program watches the drop-off lanes from above, works out which spots are full, and sends the PLC the outermost free spot in each lane. It replaced the robot's floor-level sensing, where the robot drove up to each lane and checked the spots itself.

How

Step 1 of 6

Watch the lanes

A camera on the roof trusses streams live video of the drop-off lanes.

The program reads the stream over RTSP. A separate thread keeps only the newest frame and reconnects if the stream drops, so detection always runs on current video. The program runs unattended on a maintenance computer nearby that's on around the clock.

Step 2 of 6

Get a bird's-eye view

From the trusses, the camera sees the lanes at an angle, and its lens bends straight lines.

The program first corrects the lens distortion. Then a homography, set from four points on the floor, warps the image so the lanes look as if seen from straight above, with every spot the same size.

Step 3 of 6

Find the racks

The racks are picked out by their colour.

Each frame is converted to HSV, which describes a colour's hue separately from how bright it is, so changes in lighting matter less. Pixels within the racks' colour range are marked as rack.

Step 4 of 6

Check each spot

A grid over each lane splits it into spots.

A spot counts as full when the share of rack-coloured pixels in it passes a threshold I can tune. Where the lighting makes that unreliable, a second mode compares each spot with readings saved earlier instead.

Step 5 of 6

Send the free spot

For each lane, the program works out the outermost free spot and sends it to the PLC.

It writes the spots over EtherNet/IP, along with the heartbeat bit that flips on every write so the PLC knows the program is still running. Writes are spaced out and reconnect on their own, so a brief network drop doesn't stop the system.

Step 6 of 6

Tune it on the floor

I built a calibration screen for setting the system up on site.

It adjusts the colour ranges, the lens correction, the four floor points for the bird's-eye view, the lane grids and the detection threshold, all on the live video. The settings are saved to files, so a calibrated setup loads again when the program restarts.

Impact

The robot drives straight to a free spot instead of checking each lane with its own sensors, a check it now only falls back on if the camera system stops. Its drop-off choices are based on which spots are actually free right now.

What

Touchscreen panels for machines and robots around the plant. They show live servo speed and torque for the sonic weld robots that install clips, count the screws and clips installed, and let operators skip the camera-based defect check for specific screws.

How

I built them in FactoryTalk View Studio and Studio 5000. Ladder logic in the Allen-Bradley PLC records production and diagnostic data as it happens and passes it to the screens through structured tags and arrays. I laid the screens out so operators and maintenance staff can read a machine's status at a glance, without digging through raw PLC data.

Impact

Seeing how the robots were performing made faults quicker to diagnose and maintenance easier to plan. Tool use is tracked, so worn parts get replaced before they fail, which cuts unexpected downtime. The bypasses also stopped the line from halting when it didn't need to, while the quality checks stayed in place.

Hand Tracking Computer Vision Guitar

Watch Demo

Note triggers with live chord diagram

Swipe gesture to switch chord groups

Pinch-controlled volume slider

What

A guitar you play in the air. It reads hand and finger movements from a webcam in real time and turns them into music: the left hand picks chords by finger position, and the right hand plays individual notes. Chord diagrams and fingertip markers are drawn over the live feed.

How

I wrote custom hand-tracking logic on top of MediaPipe that compares landmark positions to work out which fingers are raised or pressed. Each gesture maps to a chord or note audio file played through Pygame. Sound only plays on a new finger press, so holding a position doesn't retrigger it, and I tuned the frame processing to keep latency low on the live feed.

Impact

This was my first real-time computer vision project, and it showed me a standard webcam could drive an accurate, low-latency interactive system. What I learned here about frame processing, gesture mapping, and staying real-time went straight into the AMR vision system at Toyota Boshoku.

Autonomous Checkers Robot

Initial concept

First functional prototype

Final design

What

A robot that makes a checkers move on its own. It scans the board with optical and distance sensors, finds the pieces, picks a legal move, and makes it with a motorized claw on an X-Y gantry. The whole thing had to fit strict hardware and size limits.

How

The C++ firmware stores the board as a 2D array and handles piece detection, legal move generation, promotion, and game-over detection. The X-Y system moves the claw over the target square, and the claw picks up and places the pieces. Getting it working took several prototypes: stiffening the chassis, fixing jams, realigning the claw and sensors, and finally modifying the checkers pieces so the sensors would detect them reliably.

Impact

The final robot could detect the pieces, pick a legal move, and make it on the physical board, which needed the mechanical design, sensors, and embedded software all working together. It taught me how to iterate on a prototype and how to track down problems that could sit anywhere between the hardware and the code.

Gameplay

What

A 2D survival shooter I wrote from scratch in Java with JavaFX. You fly a spaceship, dodge randomly spawned asteroids, shoot aliens, and try to last as long as you can. How

What

I trained a reinforcement learning policy in NVIDIA Isaac Lab that gets an SO-ARM101 arm to push a block up a ramp and into a raised container using contact alone. The gripper was locked shut, so the policy couldn't grab or lift the block and had to do everything by pushing. It was built to transfer to the physical SO-101 arm later.

How

I designed the rewards to encourage the right approach position and progress up the ramp, and to penalize any attempt to lift the block. Training ran in three stages, each building on the one before:

Tactile Sensor Array

Velostat Arduino Python Tactile Sensing
Build Guide

What

With my teammates, I built a flexible tactile sensor, a skin that lets a robot feel touch. It's a 32×16 grid of pressure points, and a laptop shows a live heatmap of where and how hard it's pressed. The plan is to put it on our humanoid's hand. I finished it at the end of my study term and will pick it back up when I'm back on campus.

How

We bought the materials and built it by following FlexiTac, an open-source build guide. A sheet of Velostat sits between two flexible circuits whose lines cross, and each crossing is one pressure point. An Arduino reads the grid one row at a time and streams every reading to the laptop over USB. The guide's Python script then turns those readings into the heatmap.
